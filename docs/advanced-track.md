# The advanced track, levels 11 to 20

Levels 1 to 10 take somebody from no Python to a deployed app. They are enough to
get an internship interview. They are not enough to get hired into a fintech
engineering or risk team, because every one of those builds runs in a notebook
against a file, and the job is about systems that hold money under concurrency,
models that have to be defended, and services other people depend on.

This track is the second half. Each level is one project a reviewer can open, run
and ask questions about, and each one is chosen because the skill it proves shows
up in fintech job descriptions rather than because it is fun to build.

## What a member can say afterwards

| Level | The build | What it proves to a hiring manager |
|---|---|---|
| 11 | **Ledger on Postgres**: double entry in a real database, with the balance invariant enforced by the schema and two writers hitting the same account | ACID transactions, isolation levels, constraints as a safety net, and why an application check is not a guarantee |
| 12 | **Payment API**: FastAPI, idempotency keys that survive a restart, a payment state machine, and signed webhooks with replay protection | REST design, HMAC verification, retries, the failure cases interviewers ask about |
| 13 | **Event sourced money**: an append only event log with projections, replay from zero, and a reconciliation that proves the projection matches the log | Event sourcing, CQRS, auditability, rebuilding state after a bug |
| 14 | **Credit scorecard**: weight of evidence binning, information value, a logistic scorecard in points, reason codes, and a fairness section | The technique credit risk teams actually use, plus model documentation |
| 15 | **Fraud service in production**: online features with a latency budget, thresholds tuned on money, shadow mode, and drift monitoring | Serving a model rather than training one, and the operational half nobody teaches |
| 16 | **Backtest engine**: vectorised, with costs and slippage, walk forward validation, and a written list of the biases it avoids | Quant research hygiene: lookahead, survivorship, overfitting, and how you would know |
| 17 | **Portfolio and risk engine**: covariance shrinkage, mean variance with constraints, historical and Monte Carlo VaR, factor exposure | Portfolio construction and the risk numbers a desk reports daily |
| 18 | **Open banking aggregator**: OAuth2 with consent, token refresh, rate limits, normalising three banks into one schema, and a categoriser | The integration work most fintech backend roles are actually made of |
| 19 | **AML monitoring**: sanctions screening with fuzzy matching, a rule engine, alert triage, case files, and tuning against a false positive budget | RegTech, which hires heavily and is under taught |
| 20 | **Ship it properly**: containers, CI, tests, metrics, logs, traces, a load test, an SLO and a cost estimate for the level 12 service | That you can put a service in front of other people and keep it up |

## Rules the track keeps

Every level still has to obey the rules that made the first ten work.

- **The build uses only what the level taught**, plus anything from an earlier
  level. If a requirement needs a tool that no level introduced, that is a bug in
  the level.
- **Every dataset is synthetic**, generated by a script in `data/`, and no real
  personal or financial record is ever committed.
- **Every number in the material is verified** before it is published, against the
  dataset it describes.
- **A solution key exists** for each build, written and run before the level ships.

Two things do change.

- **Local tooling is assumed.** Level 9 set up an editor and a virtual
  environment; this track uses them. Levels 11 to 20 run on a machine, not in a
  browser tab, and several need Docker.
- **The reviewer is a hiring manager.** Each build ends with a README written for
  somebody who has ten minutes, and a limitations section, because that is what
  separates a student project from an engineering one.

## Order and dependencies

11 to 13 are one arc: the ledger, the API in front of it, and the event log under
it. They are worth doing in order.

14 and 15 are the modelling arc and assume levels 6 and 8.

16 and 17 are the markets arc and assume level 7.

18 and 19 stand alone and can be taken in either order.

20 comes last, because it puts the level 12 service into production and needs
something worth deploying.
